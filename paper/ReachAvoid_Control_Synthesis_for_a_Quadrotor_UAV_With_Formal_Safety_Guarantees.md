# Reach-Avoid Control Synthesis for a Quadrotor UAV With Formal Safety Guarantees 

Mohamed Serry ${ }^{\text {(iD) }}$, Yating Yuan ${ }^{\text {(iD) }}$, Haocheng Chang ${ }^{\text {(iD) }}$, and Jun Liu ${ }^{\text {(iD) }}$, Senior Member, IEEE


#### Abstract

Accomplishing reach-avoid tasks for quadrotor unmanned aerial vehicles while ensuring formal safety guarantees remains a challenging problem despite significant research advancements. In this article, we address this challenge by adopting an efficient planning-tracking paradigm that integrates geometric control for tracking and Bézier curves for polynomial trajectory generation. Our framework explicitly addresses tracking errors during trajectory synthesis by incorporating closed-form, timevarying error bounds that are independent of the specific trajectory, as long as it meets easily imposed constraints. This approach enables efficient trajectory synthesis while ensuring formal safety guarantees. To derive tight timevarying tracking error bounds, we revisit the stability analysis of the closed-loop quadrotor system under geometric tracking control. Our analysis demonstrates that the tracking error dynamics exhibit local exponential stability for any positive control gains. In addition, we establish sufficient conditions on the desired trajectory, utilizing the derived bounds to ensure the well-posedness of the closed-loop system. We propose a novel and efficient algorithm for constructing a safe tube or corridor using sampling-based planning and safe hyperrectangular set computations, explicitly incorporating the time-varying bounds. The trajectory is then synthesized within this safe tube while considering the error bounds, using a heuristic and computationally efficient approach based on linear programming. To validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, we present numerical simulations of a reach-avoid control task in a cluttered environment and compare the performance of our method against two state-of-the-art optimization-based tracking approaches.

## I. Introduction

QUADROTOR unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have been a focal point in control research due to their versatility in applications, such as surveying and delivery [1]. A key challenge in quadrotor applications is reaching specific targets while avoiding obstacles, necessitating the development of safe path planning and control algorithms. Standard approaches in the literature construct piecewise-continuous polynomial trajectories and leverage the differential flatness of quadrotor UAVs to design controllers that track these trajectories [2]. Trajectories are typically generated by connecting waypoints (obtained using sampling-based methods, such as rapidly exploring random tree (RRT) [3], or node-based methods, such as A* [4]) with polynomial interpolants, such as p-splines or Bernstein polynomials. Various control strategies can then be employed for trajectory tracking, including linearization-based methods [5], [6], feedback-linearization approaches [7], [8], backstepping and sliding-mode control [9], model predictive control [10], [11], and nonlinear feedback control on the special Euclidean group [12], [13]. A review of existing quadrotor control methods can be found in [14]. Despite the success of the aforementioned planning-tracking paradigm, it typically lacks formal guarantees in the following sense: if the quadrotor's initial state does not precisely match that of the planned trajectory, then there is no assurance that the controller will achieve tracking without violating safety constraints.

Guaranteeing safety during reach-avoid control scenarios is generally challenging. One approach that provides formal guarantees is abstraction-based control synthesis [15], [16], [17], [18], which is practically inapplicable to the high-dimensional quadrotor dynamics as the computational requirements of that approach increase exponentially with the system dimension.

Another relevant approach that can, in principle, provide safety guarantees is based on control contraction metrics (CCMs) [19]. In this line of work, robust invariant sets are estimated under nonlinear feedback control, and outer bounds of these sets are incorporated into the synthesis of nominal trajectories to be tracked [20], [21], [22]. However, computing such invariant-set approximations is computationally demanding and requires polynomial approximations of the quadrotor dynamics. Moreover, evaluating a CCM controller necessitates estimating minimizing geodesics, which cannot be computed exactly. As a result, the formal guarantees of this approach are weakened in practice. It is also worth noting that CCM-based methods for quadrotor applications have, to date, been demonstrated only on reduced-order models (e.g., a 2-D quadrotor model or a 3-D quadrotor model with angular rates used as control inputs).

Control barrier functions (CBFs) [23] offer another compelling control methodology with potential safety guarantees.

In [24] and [25], candidate CBFs are embedded into quadrotor reach-avoid tasks, with control inputs computed online via quadratic programs. However, CBF-based approaches typically assume reduced-order quadrotor dynamics in which angular velocities are treated as control inputs, leaving the task of tracking these desired angular velocities to a low-level controller whose formal correctness is not established. In addition, the smoothness of optimization-based controllers obtained from control barrier function-based quadratic program (CBF-QPs) is not inherently guaranteed, creating challenges for real-world deployment.

Another related approach, based on computing forward and backward reachable sets, was proposed in [26], where trajectory generation is integrated with reachability computations. Although this method provides formal safety guarantees, it is computationally demanding. Furthermore, similar to the work in [24] and [25], it models angular velocities as control inputs, which may further compromise safety assurances when applied to the full quadrotor dynamics.

These limitations collectively highlight the need for further research on reach-avoid control synthesis for quadrotor systems that ensures rigorous and practically verifiable safety guarantees.

In this work, we propose a variation of the standard planningtracking paradigm that explicitly accounts for tracking errors during trajectory planning. Specifically, we adopt geometric control (GC) [13], [27], [28] for tracking and revisit its stability analysis to derive tight, time-varying error bounds. These bounds are independent of the UAV trajectory, provided it satisfies certain easily imposed conditions, enabling efficient nominal trajectory synthesis.

Interestingly, our stability analysis of GC reveals that the tracking error dynamics exhibit local exponential stability for any choice of positive control gains. In addition, we establish sufficient conditions on the generated trajectories to prevent singularities in the tracking controller, ensuring the well-posedness of the closed-loop system, an aspect previously overlooked in the literature.

While alternative tracking methods (e.g., [11] and [12]) offer stronger theoretical guarantees, such as almost global asymptotic stability, integrating them into a planning framework with formal safety guarantees remains challenging, as deriving closed-form tracking error bounds for such controllers is practically infeasible.

For trajectory synthesis, we introduce an efficient samplingbased planning algorithm that uses safe hyperrectangular set computations to generate a safe tube or corridor, which is then used for polynomial trajectory generation. The safe tube construction explicitly accounts for the time-varying tracking error bounds. In addition, we propose a heuristic, computationally efficient approach for computing a safe polynomial trajectory (represented as a piecewise-continuous Bézier curve) that remains within the generated safe tube. The error bounds are integrated into the synthesis procedure to ensure compliance with safety specifications, including thrust and velocity constraints.

The organization of this article is as follows. Mathematical preliminaries are introduced in Section II. The quadrotor model equations are presented in Section III. The setup for the reachavoid control synthesis problem is provided in Section IV. The geometric tracking control and the tracking errors are introduced in Section V. Stability of the closed-loop system is discussed in Section VI. The trajectory generation procedure is presented in detail in Section VII. The performance of our approach is displayed through numerical simulations in Section VIII. The study is concluded in Section IX.

## II. Preliminaries

Let $\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}_{+}, \mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{Z}_{+}$denote the sets of real numbers, nonnegative real numbers, integers, and nonnegative integers, respectively, and $\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{Z}_{+} \backslash\{0\}$. Let $\left.[a, b],\right] a, b[,[a, b[$, and $] a, b]$ denote the closed, open, and half-open intervals, respectively, with endpoints $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, and $[a ; b]], a ; b[,[a ; b[$, and $] a ; b]$ stand for their discrete counterparts, e.g., $[a ; b]=[a, b] \cap \mathbb{Z}$ and $[1 ; 4[=\{1,2,3\}$.

In $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, the relations $<, \leq, \geq$, and $>$ are defined componentwise. For $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n},|x| \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is defined as $(|x|)_{i}:=\left|x_{i}\right|, i \in$ $[1 ; n]$. The $n$-dimensional vectors with zero and unit entries are denoted by $0_{n}$ and $1_{n}$, respectively. Given $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^{n}, x \cdot y$ denotes the standard inner product of $x$ and $y$. For 3-D vectors $x$ and $y, x \times y$ denotes the cross product of $x$ and $y$. We equip $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ with the Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|$ and we make use of the maximal norm $\|\cdot\|_{\infty}$.

The cardinality of a finite set $M$ is denoted by $\operatorname{card}(M)$. The convex hull of $x_{1}, x_{2}, \ldots, x_{N} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is denoted by $\operatorname{conv}\left(x_{1}, x_{2}, \ldots, x_{N}\right)$. The $n$-dimensional closed unit balls with respect to $\|\cdot\|$ and $\|\cdot\|_{\infty}$ are denoted by $\mathbb{B}_{n}$ and $\mathbb{B}_{n}^{\infty}$, respectively, (note that $\mathbb{B}_{n} \subseteq \mathbb{B}_{n}^{\infty}$ ). For $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$, with $a \leq b$, the hyperrectangle $\llbracket a, b \rrbracket$ denotes the set $\left\{x \in \mathbb{R}^{n} \mid a \leq x \leq b\right\}$, where $\operatorname{center}(\llbracket a, b \rrbracket):=(a+$ $b) / 2$ and $\operatorname{radius}(\llbracket a, b \rrbracket):=(b-a) / 2$. Given $M, N \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n}$, $\operatorname{int}(M)$ denotes the interior of $M, M \backslash N:=\{x \in M \mid x \notin$ $N\}, M+N:=\{y+z \mid y \in M, z \in N\}$, and $M-N:=$ $\left\{z \in \mathbb{R}^{n} \mid z+N \subseteq M\right\}$ (see, e.g., [29]). The following technical result will be essential for ensuring trajectory safety in our framework.

Lemma 1: Let $X, Y, Z \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n}$ be nonempty sets, and assume that $Z$ is centrally symmetric about the origin (i.e., $z \in$ $Z \Leftrightarrow-z \in Z), X-Z$ is nonempty, and $(X-Z) \backslash(Y+Z)$ is nonempty. Then, $Z+(X-Z) \backslash(Y+Z) \subseteq X \backslash Y$.

Proof: Let $m \in Z+(X-Z) \backslash(Y+Z)$. Then, there exist $n \in Z$ and $p \in(X-Z) \backslash(Y+Z)$ with $m=n+p$. Since $p \in$ $X-Z, p+z \in X$ for all $z \in Z$, implying $m=p+n \in X$. Because $p \notin Y+Z$, there is no pair $(y, z) \in Y \times Z$ such that $p=y+z$. Using the central symmetry of $Z$, the condition $p=$ $y+z$ is equivalent to $y=p-z=p+\tilde{z}$, where $\tilde{z}=-z \in Z$. Thus, no element of the form $p+z$ with $z \in Z$ belongs to $Y$, i.e., $(p+Z) \cap Y=\emptyset$. Since $m \in p+Z, m \notin Y$. $\square$

The $n \times n$ identity matrix and the $n \times m$ zero matrix are denoted by $\mathrm{I}_{n}$ and $0_{n \times m}$, respectively. The transpose of an $n \times m$ matrix $A$ is denoted by $A^{\top}$. The trace and the determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ are denoted by $\operatorname{tr}(A)$ and $\operatorname{det}(A)$, respectively. Given $d=\left[d_{1} \cdots d_{n}\right]^{\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$, the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries $d_{1}, \ldots, d_{n}$ is denoted by $\operatorname{diag}(d)$. For $n \times m$ real matrices, $\|\cdot\|$ is the matrix norm induced by the Euclidean norm.

Positive Definite Matrices: Let $\mathcal{S}^{n}$ denote the space of $n \times n$ real symmetric matrices. Given $A \in \mathcal{S}^{n}, \underline{\lambda}(A)$ and $\bar{\lambda}(A)$ denote the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of $A$, respectively. Let $\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}$ denote the set of $n \times n$ real symmetric positive definite matrices. Given $A \in \mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}, A^{\frac{1}{2}}$ denotes the unique real symmetric positive definite matrix $K$ satisfying $A=K^{2}$ [30, p. 220], and we define $A^{-\frac{1}{2}}:=\left(A^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{-1}$. The following lemma
states some useful estimates associated with symmetric positive definite matrices.

Lemma 2: For all $M, W \in \mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}, x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$, and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$

(a) $\underline{\lambda}(M)\|x\|^{2} \leq x^{\boldsymbol{\top}} M x \leq \bar{\lambda}(M)\|x\|^{2}$,
(b) $\underline{\lambda}\left(M^{-\frac{1}{2}} W M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left\|M^{\frac{1}{2}} x\right\|^{2} \leq\left\|W^{\frac{1}{2}} x\right\|^{2}$,
(c) $\|A x\| \leq\left\|A M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|\left\|M^{\frac{1}{2}} x\right\|$.

Proof: For (a), as $M$ is symmetric, it is diagonalizable, i.e., $M=Q^{\top} D Q$, where $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is orthogonal, and $D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the positive eigenvalues of $M, \lambda_{1}, \ldots, \lambda_{n}$, i.e., $D=\operatorname{diag}\left(\left[\lambda_{1} \cdots \lambda_{n}\right]^{\top}\right)$. Let $y=Q x$. Then, $\underline{\lambda}(M)\|y\|^{2} \leq x^{\top} M x=y^{\top} D y=\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_{i} y_{i}^{2} \leq$ $\bar{\lambda}(M)\|y\|^{2}$. As $Q$ is orthogonal, $\|y\|=\|x\|$, and (a) follows. For (b), first note that as $M \in \mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}$, its square root $M^{\frac{1}{2}}$ is also in $\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}$. Moreover, $M^{-\frac{1}{2}} \in \mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}$ and, consequently, $M^{-\frac{1}{2}} W M^{-\frac{1}{2}} \in \mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}$ (see [30, p. 222]). Therefore, utilizing (a), we have $\left\|W^{\frac{1}{2}} x\right\|^{2}=x^{\top} W x=x^{\top} M^{\frac{1}{2}} M^{-\frac{1}{2}} W M^{-\frac{1}{2}} M^{\frac{1}{2}} x=$ $\left(M^{\frac{1}{2}} x\right)^{\top}\left(M^{-\frac{1}{2}} W M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left(M^{\frac{1}{2}} x\right) \geq \underline{\lambda}\left(M^{-\frac{1}{2}} W M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left\|M^{\frac{1}{2}} x\right\|^{2}$. The estimate in (c) follows as $\|A x\|=\left\|A M^{-\frac{1}{2}} M^{\frac{1}{2}} x\right\| \leq$ $\left\|A M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|\left\|M^{\frac{1}{2}} x\right\|$. $\square$

Special Orthogonal Group: Let SO(3) denote the Lie group of real $3 \times 3$ proper orthogonal matrices and $\mathfrak{s o}(3)$ denote the Lie algebra of real 3 × 3 skew-symmetric matrices. The hat map $\wedge: \mathbb{R}^{3} \rightarrow \mathfrak{s o}(3)$ is defined such that $\hat{x} y=x \times y$ for all $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$, and the vee map $\vee: \mathfrak{s o}(3) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{3}$ is the inverse of $\wedge$.

A key representation result concerning the $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ group is the following (see, e.g., [31] and [32]):

Lemma 3 (Euler-Rodrigues formula): For each $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, there exists $x \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ such that

$$
R=\exp (\hat{x})= \begin{cases}\mathrm{I}_{3}+\frac{\sin (\|x\|)}{\|x\|} \hat{x}+\frac{1-\cos (\|x\|)}{\|x\|^{2}} \hat{x}^{2}, & x \neq 0_{3} \\ \mathrm{I}_{3}, & x=0_{3}\end{cases}
$$

Bézier Curves: An $n$-dimensional Bézier curve (see, e.g., [33] and [34]) $\mathfrak{B}:[0, T] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n}$, of one segment, with $N+1$ control points, is given by

$$
\mathfrak{B}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N} \mathfrak{c}^{i} \mathfrak{b}_{i, N}\left(\frac{t}{T}\right), t \in[0, T]
$$

where $\mathfrak{c}^{i} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}, i \in[0 ; N]$, are the control points determining the shape of the curve, and $\mathfrak{b}_{i, N}:[0,1] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, i \in[0 ; N]$ are the Bernstein polynomials defined as

$$
\mathfrak{b}_{i, N}(s):=\frac{N!}{i!(N-i)!} s^{i}(1-s)^{N-i}, s \in[0,1], i \in[0 ; N]
$$

where we define $0^{0}:=1$. The Bézier curve $\mathfrak{B}$ satisfies

$$
\mathfrak{B}(0)=\mathfrak{c}^{0}, \mathfrak{B}(T)=\mathfrak{c}^{N}, \mathfrak{B}(t) \in \operatorname{conv}\left(\mathfrak{c}^{0}, \ldots, \mathfrak{c}^{N}\right), t \in[0, T] .
$$

The derivatives of Bézier curves are also Bézier curves. For example, $\dot{\mathfrak{B}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(N / T)\left(\mathfrak{c}^{i+1}-\mathfrak{c}^{i}\right) \mathfrak{b}_{i, N-1}(t / T)$, $\ddot{\mathfrak{B}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\left(N(N-1) / T^{2}\right)\left(\mathfrak{c}^{i+2}-2 \mathfrak{c}^{i+1}+\mathfrak{c}^{i}\right) \mathfrak{b}_{i, N-2}$ $(t / T), \dddot{\mathfrak{B}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-3}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{2}(N-j) / T^{3}\right)\left(\mathfrak{c}^{i+3}-3 \mathfrak{c}^{i+2}+\right.$ $\left.3 \mathfrak{c}^{i+1}-\mathfrak{c}^{i}\right) \mathfrak{b}_{i, N-3}(t / T)$, and $\dddot{\mathfrak{B}}(t)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-4}\left(\prod_{j=0}^{3}(N-j) /\right.$ $\left.T^{4}\right)\left(\mathfrak{c}^{i+4}-4 \mathfrak{c}^{i+3}+6 \mathfrak{c}^{i+2}-4 \mathfrak{c}^{i+1}+\mathfrak{c}^{i}\right) \mathfrak{b}_{i, N-4}(t / T), t \in[0, T]$. Therefore, the ranges of $\mathfrak{B}$ and its derivatives can be controlled by tuning the control points $\mathfrak{c}^{0}, \ldots, \mathfrak{c}^{N}$.

## III. Quadrotor Model

Let $\left\{e_{1}, e_{2}, e_{3}\right\}$ be the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^{3}$, which corresponds to the inertial reference (world) frame, where $e_{3}$ is pointing upward. Let $I \subset \mathbb{R}_{+}$be an interval with 0 as the left endpoint, and let $t \in I$. The moving fixed-body frame of the quadrotor system is given by the time-varying orthonormal basis $\left\{b_{1}(t), b_{2}(t), b_{3}(t)\right\}$. The rotation matrix (attitude) that transforms the fixed-body frame into the inertial frame is $R(t) \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, where $b_{i}(t)=R(t) e_{i}, i \in[1 ; 3]$. The mass of the quadrotor is $m \in \mathbb{R}_{+} \backslash\{0\}$ and its inertia matrix, with respect to the moving fixed-body frame, is $J \in \mathcal{S}_{++}^{3}$. The quadrotor system consists of four identical rotors and propellers, with each pair capable of generating thrust and torque independently. The total thrust generated by the propellers is $f(t) \in \mathbb{R}$, and the total generated torque is $\tau(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$. The position of the quadrotor's center of mass is $p(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$, its velocity is $v(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$, and the quadrotor's angular velocity, with respect to the fixedbody frame, is $\omega(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$. Let $g \in \mathbb{R}_{+}$denote the gravitational acceleration. Assuming negligible aerodynamic drag forces, the governing equations are

$$
\begin{align*}
& \dot{p}(t)=v(t)  \tag{1}\\
& \dot{v}(t)=-g e_{3}+m^{-1} f(t) R(t) e_{3}  \tag{2}\\
& \dot{R}(t)=R(t) \hat{\omega}(t)  \tag{3}\\
& \dot{\omega}(t)=J^{-1}(-\omega(t) \times J \omega(t)+\tau(t)), t \in I \tag{4}
\end{align*}
$$

## IV. Problem Formulation

Let $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{o}}=\llbracket \underline{x}_{\mathrm{o}}, \bar{x}_{\mathrm{o}} \rrbracket \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{3}$ be a bounded hyperrectangular operating domain and $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{u}}} \llbracket \underline{x}_{\mathrm{u}}^{(i)}, \bar{x}_{\mathrm{u}}^{(i)} \rrbracket \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{3}$ be an unsafe set defined as a union of $N_{\mathrm{u}}$ hyperrectangles. It is required that the quadrotor's position is always inside the operating domain, while avoiding the unsafe set. Let $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{t}}=\llbracket \underline{x}_{\mathrm{t}}, \bar{x}_{\mathrm{t}} \rrbracket \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{3}$ be a hyperrectangular target set that we aim to drive the quadrotor's position into, where we assume $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{t}} \subseteq \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{o}} \backslash \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}$.

Given a velocity bound $v_{\text {max }} \in \mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}$, a thrust bound $f_{\text {max }} \in$ $\mathbb{R}_{+}$, a nominal initial point $\left(p_{0}, v_{0}, R_{0}, \omega_{0}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{3} \times \mathbb{R}^{3} \times$ $\mathrm{SO}(3) \times \mathbb{R}^{3}$, where it is assumed that $p_{0} \in \operatorname{int}\left(\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{o}}\right) \backslash \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}},\left|v_{0}\right|<$ $v_{\text {max }}$, and $\left(R_{0}, \omega_{0}\right)=\left(\mathrm{I}_{3}, 0_{3}\right)$, find force and torque control laws such that there exist a finite time $T>0$ and a nonsingleton subset of $\mathbb{R}^{3} \times \mathbb{R}^{3} \times \operatorname{SO}(3) \times \mathbb{R}^{3}$ containing $\left(p_{0}, v_{0}, R_{0}, \omega_{0}\right)$ in its relative interior, where for any initial value of the quadrotor system ( $p(0), v(0), R(0), \omega(0)$ ) in that set, the corresponding trajectory of the quadrotor system and associated thrust satisfy

$$
\begin{align*}
& p(t) \in \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{o}} \backslash \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}},|v(t)| \leq v_{\max },|f(t)| \leq f_{\max } \quad \forall t \in[0, T] \\
& p(T) \in \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{t}} . \tag{5}
\end{align*}
$$

To address the problem under consideration, our approach designs a desired trajectory alongside a tracking controller, ensuring that the trajectory explicitly accounts for tight, time-varying bounds that overestimate the tracking errors. These bounds are independent of the trajectory, provided it satisfies certain easily imposed conditions. We first introduce the tracking controller and rigorously analyze its stability properties to derive tracking error bounds, adapting the GC framework from [13].

## V. Geometric Control

Let $I \subset \mathbb{R}_{+}$be an interval with 0 as the left endpoint, and let $t \in I$. For tracking a desired trajectory, with desired position $p_{d}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$, rotation matrix $R_{d}(t) \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, and angular velocity $\omega_{d}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$, we define [13]

$$
\begin{equation*}
e_{p}(t):=p(t)-p_{d}(t) \tag{6}
\end{equation*}
$$

$$
\begin{align*}
e_{v}(t) & :=v(t)-\dot{p}_{d}(t)  \tag{7}\\
e_{R}(t) & :=\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{d}^{\top}(t) R(t)-R^{\top}(t) R_{d}(t)\right)^{\vee}  \tag{8}\\
\Psi(t) & :=\frac{1}{2} \operatorname{tr}\left(\mathrm{I}_{3}-R_{d}^{\top}(t) R(t)\right)  \tag{9}\\
e_{\omega}(t) & :=\omega(t)-R^{\top}(t) R_{d}(t) \omega_{d}(t) \tag{10}
\end{align*}
$$

where $e_{p}, e_{v}, e_{R}$, and $e_{\omega}$ are the error functions of the position, velocity, attitude, and angular velocity, respectively, and $\Psi$ is the configuration error function [13], [35]. We adopt the GC law in [35] given by

$$
\begin{align*}
f(t)= & F_{d}(t) \cdot R(t) e_{3}  \tag{11}\\
F_{d}(t)= & -k_{p} e_{p}(t)-k_{v} e_{v}(t)+m g e_{3}+m \ddot{p}_{d}(t)  \tag{12}\\
\tau(t)= & -k_{R} e_{R}(t)-k_{\omega} e_{\omega}(t)+\omega(t) \times J \omega(t) \\
& -J\left(\hat{\omega}(t) R^{\top}(t) R_{d}(t) \omega_{d}(t)-R^{\top}(t) R_{d}(t) \dot{\omega}_{d}(t)\right) \tag{13}
\end{align*}
$$

where $k_{p}, k_{v}, k_{R}$, and $k_{\omega}$ are positive control gains. As our problem is concerned with maneuvering within an operating domain to reach a target set while avoiding obstacles, the desired attitude and angular velocity should ensure tracking the desired position $p_{d}$ successfully. As in [35] and [36], we set

$$
\begin{align*}
R_{d}(t) & =\left[b_{1, d}(t), b_{2, d}(t), b_{3, d}(t)\right]  \tag{14}\\
\hat{\omega}_{d}(t) & =R_{d}^{\top}(t) \dot{R}_{d}(t)  \tag{15}\\
b_{1, d}(t) & =\frac{1}{\left\|F_{d}(t)\right\|}\left(\begin{array}{c}
F_{d, 3}(t)+\frac{\left(F_{d, 2}(t)\right)^{2}}{\left\|F_{d}(t)\right\|+F_{d, 3}(t)} \\
-\frac{F_{d, 1}(t) F_{d, 2}(t)}{\left\|F_{d}(t)\right\|+F_{d, 3}(t)} \\
-F_{d, 1}(t)
\end{array}\right) \\
b_{3, d}(t) & =\frac{F_{d}(t)}{\left\|F_{d}(t)\right\|}, b_{2, d}(t)=-b_{1, d}(t) \times b_{3, d}(t) \tag{16}
\end{align*}
$$

The derivatives of the error functions $e_{p}, e_{v}, \Psi, e_{R}$, and $e_{\omega}$, after embedding the GC, are given by (see, e.g., [37])

$$
\begin{align*}
\dot{e}_{p}(t) & =e_{v}(t)  \tag{17}\\
\dot{e}_{v}(t) & =-\frac{1}{m}\left(k_{p} e_{p}(t)+k_{v} e_{v}(t)-\Delta_{f}(t)\right)  \tag{18}\\
\dot{\Psi}(t) & =e_{R}(t) \cdot e_{\omega}(t)  \tag{19}\\
\dot{e}_{R}(t) & =\mathcal{C}(t) e_{\omega}(t)  \tag{20}\\
\dot{e}_{\omega}(t) & =J^{-1}\left(-k_{R} e_{R}(t)-k_{\omega} e_{\omega}(t)\right) \tag{21}
\end{align*}
$$

where

$$
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{C}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{tr}\left[R^{\top}(t) R_{d}(t)\right] \mathrm{I}_{3}-R^{\top}(t) R_{d}(t)\right) \tag{22}
\end{equation*}
$$

and

$$
\begin{equation*}
\Delta_{f}(t):=\left\|F_{d}(t)\right\|\left(\left(b_{3, d}(t) \cdot b_{3}(t)\right) b_{3}(t)-b_{3, d}(t)\right) . \tag{23}
\end{equation*}
$$

The derivative of $F_{d}$, given by (12), after substituting (17) and (18) in is given by

$$
\begin{equation*}
\dot{F}_{d}(t)=-k_{p} e_{v}(t)-\frac{k_{v}}{m}\left(-k_{p} e_{p}(t)-k_{v} e_{v}(t)+\Delta_{f}(t)\right)+\dddot{p}_{d}(t) . \tag{24}
\end{equation*}
$$

We fix the time interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}_{+}$, assume that 0 is the left endpoint of $I$, and consider the closed-loop quadrotor system (1)-(4), with (6)-(10) and (11)-(16), over $I$ throughout the discussion below. Unless otherwise specified, we assume that the control gains are fixed. In addition, it is assumed that $p_{d}$ is four-times continuously differentiable over $I$, where $p_{d}$ and its first four derivatives are bounded over $I$.

Note that in the definition of $\hat{\omega}_{d}$ in (15), we differentiate $R_{d}$ with respect to time, which necessarily requires differentiating $F_{d}$ [see (14) and (16)], where the evolution equations of $p$ and $v$, given by (1) and (2), respectively, are used. Note also that $\omega_{d}$ depends on $\dddot{p}_{d}$ and that $\dot{\omega}_{d}$, which is used in the torque law (13), depends on $\dddot{p}_{d}$, hence the four-times continuous differentiability assumption imposed on $p_{d}$.

In light of (16), it is necessary to guarantee that

$$
\begin{equation*}

\end{equation*}
$$

for all $t \in I$, in order to ensure that the closed-loop dynamics remain well-defined. In Section VI-B, we will introduce sufficient conditions on $p_{d}$ that ensure existence [i.e., the functions $p, v, R$, and $\omega$, defined through the closed-loop system (1)-(4), with (6)- (10) and (11)-(16), do in fact exist over $I$ ] and well-definedness [condition (25) holding].

In the subsequent analysis, we assume the second derivative of $p_{d}$ to satisfy

$$
\begin{equation*}
\left|g e_{3}+\ddot{p}_{d}(t)\right| \leq a_{\max } \tag{26}
\end{equation*}
$$

for all $t \in I$ and some specified $a_{\text {max }} \in \mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}$. This bound will be useful in our stability analysis and when verifying the fulfillment of the thrust bound.

## A. Local Exponential Stability

Let us state an important implication of Theorem 4. Assume $I=\mathbb{R}_{+}$. The bounding function $\mathcal{L}$ given in (46) is continuous over $\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}$ and monotonically increasing with respect to the first two arguments, where $\mathcal{L}(0,0, t)=0$ for all $t \in I$. Moreover, for fixed $x, y \in \mathbb{R}_{+}, \mathcal{L}(x, y, t) \rightarrow 0$ as $t \rightarrow \infty$. Let $x, y, t \in \mathbb{R}_{+}$and $\nu \in] 0, \alpha_{0} / 2\left[\right.$. Knowing that $\alpha_{0} / 2$, defined by (51), is less than or equal to $\beta$ given in (50), the function $\mathcal{L}_{2}$ in (45) can be bounded as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{2}(y, t) & =\mathrm{e}^{\frac{\alpha_{1} \sqrt{y}}{2 \beta}} \frac{\alpha_{2} \sqrt{y}}{2} \mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2} t} \int_{0}^{t} \mathrm{e} \overbrace{\left(\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}-\beta\right)}^{<\nu} s \\
& \leq \mathrm{e}^{\frac{\alpha_{1} \sqrt{y}}{2 \beta}} \frac{\alpha_{2} \sqrt{y}}{2 \nu} \mathrm{e}^{-\left(\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}-\nu\right) t}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can also bound $\mathcal{L}_{1}$ in (44) as follows:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{1}(x, y, t) \leq \mathrm{e}^{\frac{\alpha_{1} \sqrt{y}}{2 \beta}} \sqrt{x+y} \mathrm{e}^{-\left(\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}-\nu\right) t} .
$$

Consequently, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}(x, y, t) \leq & \exp \left(\frac{\alpha_{1} \sqrt{y}}{2 \beta}\right)\left(\sqrt{x+y}+\frac{\alpha_{2} \sqrt{y}}{2 \nu}\right) \\
& \exp \left(-\left(\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}-\nu\right) t\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, and using Theorem 4, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{V(t)} \leq & \sqrt{V(0)} \exp \left(\frac{\alpha_{1} \sqrt{V(0)}}{2 \beta}\right)\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2 \nu}\right) \\
& \exp \left(-\left(\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2}-\nu\right) t\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

for all $t \in I$. The above inequality is valid whenever $\left(e_{p}(0), e_{v}(0), e_{R}(0), e_{\omega}(0)\right)$ satisfies (27) and (28). This indicates the local exponential stability of the error dynamics. While a similar result was derived in previous works, e.g., [13], additional assumptions were imposed on the control gains and the parameters $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$. Our result herein is stronger in the sense that we have local exponential stability for any positive choice of the control gains $k_{p}, k_{v}, k_{R}$, and $k_{\omega}$.

for some $\varepsilon>0$. In this section, we use Theorem 4 to derive monotonic, time-varying bounds that can be utilized in the synthesis of $p_{d}$. Leveraging this observation, we present in the next corollary, whose proof follows

## VII. Trajectory Generation

Fix $\left.\alpha_{\psi} \in\right] 0,1[, \bar{\Psi} \in] 0,2\left[\right.$, and $\left.\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{1} \in\right] 0, \infty\left[\right.$, and let $\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{2}$ be computed via (61). We describe the computation of the desired trajectory $p_{d}$ by combining sampling-based planning [40], [41] with efficient hyperrectangular computations [42]. We first construct a safe tube of connected hyperrectangles that avoid the unsafe set while accounting for the position error bound $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}$. We then synthesize a polynomial trajectory through this tube via an optimization problem that enforces the sufficient safety and well-definedness conditions. Below, we illustrate the safe-tube construction.

## A. Computing a Safe Tube

In view of Theorem 6, define

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{o}, t}:=\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{o}}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\left(\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{1}, \overline{\mathcal{V}}_{2}, t\right) \mathbb{B}_{3}^{\infty} \\
& \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{u}, t}^{(i)}:=\llbracket \underline{x}_{\mathrm{u}}^{(i)}, \bar{x}_{\mathrm{u}}^{(i)} \rrbracket+\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\left(\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{1}, \overline{\mathcal{V}}_{2}, t\right) \mathbb{B}_{3}^{\infty}, i \in\left[1 ; N_{u}\right] \\
& \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{u}, t}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{u}}} \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{u}, t}^{(i)} \\
& \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{t}, t}:=\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{t}}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\left(\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{1}, \overline{\mathcal{V}}_{2}, t\right) \mathbb{B}_{3}^{\infty}, t \in \mathbb{R}_{+} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The sets above correspond to inflating the unsafe set and deflating the operating domain and target set, where deflation and inflation are based on the position error bound $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}$. Note that in our deflation and inflation procedure, we use the unit ball $\mathbb{B}_{3}^{\infty}$ instead of $\mathbb{B}_{3}$, which is more conservative yet computationally more efficient as Minkowski sums and differences of hyperrectangles can be computed exactly by summing/subtracting centers and radii. The safe tube is obtained by generating $N_{s}+1$ waypoints $\mathrm{p}_{0}, \mathrm{p}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{p}_{N_{s}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$, and associated vector radii $\mathrm{r}_{0}, \mathrm{r}_{1}, \mathrm{r}_{2}, \ldots, \mathrm{r}_{N_{s}} \in \mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}$, and times $\mathrm{t}_{0}=0<\mathrm{t}_{1}<\ldots<$ $\mathrm{t}_{N_{s}}<\infty$, satisfying $\forall i \in\left[0 ; N_{s}-1\right]$

$$
\begin{align*}
\mathrm{p}_{0} & =p_{0} \\
\mathrm{p}_{i+1} & \in \mathrm{p}_{i}+\llbracket-\mathrm{r}_{i}, \mathrm{r}_{i} \rrbracket \\
\mathrm{p}_{i}+\llbracket-\mathrm{r}_{i}, \mathrm{r}_{i} \rrbracket & \subseteq \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{o}, \mathrm{t}_{i}} \backslash \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{u}, \mathrm{t}_{i}} \\
\mathrm{p}_{N_{s}}+\llbracket-\mathrm{r}_{N_{s}}, \mathrm{r}_{N_{s}} \rrbracket & \subseteq \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{t}, \mathrm{t}_{N_{s}}} . \tag{73}
\end{align*}
$$

Note that if, we set $T=\mathrm{t}_{N_{s}}$, and we synthesize a trajectory $p_{d}:[0, T] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{3}$ such that

$$
\begin{align*}
p_{d}(t) & \in \mathrm{p}_{i}+\llbracket-\mathrm{r}_{i}, \mathrm{r}_{i} \rrbracket, t \in\left[\mathrm{t}_{i}, \mathrm{t}_{i+1}\right], i \in\left[0 ; N_{s-1}\right] \\
p_{d}(T) & \in \mathrm{p}_{N_{s}}+\llbracket-\mathrm{r}_{N_{s}}, \mathrm{r}_{N_{s}} \rrbracket \tag{74}
\end{align*}
$$

Next, we illustrate in the following lemmas the construction of safe hyperrectangular sets that enable forming the safe tube.

Lemma 8 (Lemma 2 in [42]): Let $v \in \llbracket a, b \rrbracket$, where $a, b \in$ $\mathbb{R}^{n}, a \leq b$, then $\mathcal{H}(v, \llbracket a, b \rrbracket) \subseteq \llbracket a, b \rrbracket$, where $\mathcal{H}(v, \llbracket a, b \rrbracket):=$ $v+\llbracket-r, r \rrbracket$, and $r=\operatorname{radius}(\llbracket a, b \rrbracket)-|\operatorname{center}(\llbracket a, b \rrbracket)-v|$.

Lemma 9 (Lemma 3 in [42]): Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $\llbracket a, b \rrbracket \subseteq$ $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^{n}, a \leq b$. Let $r=\operatorname{radius}(\llbracket a, b \rrbracket)$ and $c=\operatorname{center}(\llbracket a, b \rrbracket)$. Then, $\min _{y \in \llbracket a, b \rrbracket}\|x-y\|_{\infty}=\left\|x-y^{*}\right\|_{\infty}$, where $y^{*}=\operatorname{CP}(x, \llbracket a, b \rrbracket)$

$$
(\operatorname{CP}(x, \llbracket a, b \rrbracket))_{i}:= \begin{cases}x_{i}, & x_{i} \in\left[a_{i}, b_{i}\right] \\ c_{i}+r_{i} \operatorname{sgn}\left(x_{i}-c_{i}\right), & \text { otherwise }\end{cases}
$$

where $i \in[1 ; n]$, and $\operatorname{sgn}(\cdot)$ is the signum function. Now, assume $x \notin \llbracket a, b \rrbracket$, and let $\tilde{i} \in[1 ; p]$ such that $\left|x_{\tilde{i}}-y_{\tilde{i}}^{*}\right|=\left\|x-y^{*}\right\|_{\infty}>$ 0 , and define $\mathcal{S}(x, \llbracket a, b \rrbracket, \alpha):=\left\{z \in \mathbb{R}^{n}| | z_{\tilde{i}}-x_{\tilde{i}} \mid \leq \alpha \| x-\right.$ $\left.y^{*} \|_{\infty}\right\}$, where $\alpha \in[0,1[$. Then, $\mathcal{S}(x, \llbracket a, b \rrbracket, \alpha) \cap \llbracket a, b \rrbracket=\emptyset$.

Using Lemmas 8 and 9, the following corollary illustrates how to compute a safe hyperrectangle within an operating domain without intersecting with the unsafe set.

Corollary 4: Let $t \in \mathbb{R}_{+}, y \in \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{o}, t} \backslash \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{u}, t}$, fix $\alpha \in[0,1[$, and define $\Re\left(y, \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{o}, t}, \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{u}, t}, \alpha\right):=\mathcal{H}\left(y, \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{o}, t}\right) \cap\left(\cap_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{u}}} \mathcal{S}(y\right.$, $\left.\left.\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{u}, t}^{(i)}, \alpha\right)\right)$. Then, $\Re\left(y, \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{o}, t}, \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{u}, t}, \alpha\right) \subseteq \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{o}, t} \backslash \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{u}, t}$.

Using the safe hyperrectangles in Lemma 8 and Corollary 4, we construct a RRT $\mathscr{T}=(\mathscr{V}, \mathscr{T}, \mathscr{E})$, with a set of vertices $\mathscr{V}$, a set of associated time stamps $\mathscr{T}$, and a set of edges $\mathscr{E}$, such that each vertex (except the first one) is contained in a safe hyperrectangular neighbor of the parent vertex. The metric used in constructing the tree is as follows: the distance between a sample point $x_{s}$ and a point $x_{v}$ in $\mathscr{V}$ with an associated time stamp $t_{v}$ in the set $\mathscr{T}$ is the distance between the sample point and a safe hyperrectangle centered at $x_{v}$ (computed according to Corollary 4 at time $t_{v}$, where the distance is computed as in Lemma 9). If $x_{v}$ happens to be associated with the shortest distance (among all the vertices of the tree), then a new point $x_{\text {new }}$ is added to the tree, which is the point within the safe hyperrectangle of $x_{v}$ that corresponds to that shortest distance (see the formula of CP) and the time stamp for the new point, $t_{\text {new }}$ is computed as

$$
t_{\text {new }}=t_{\text {parent }}+\frac{\left\|x_{\text {new }}-x_{\text {parent }}\right\|}{\alpha_{v}}
$$

where $\alpha_{v}>0$ is a user-defined velocity parameter, $x_{\text {parent }}$ is the parent vertex, and $t_{\text {parent }}$ is the associated time stamp.

Let $N_{v}$ be the user-defined maximum number of vertices, $\left.\left.C_{\text {sample }} \in\right] 0,1\right]$ be a parameter that determines the percentage of points to be sampled from $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{o}} \backslash \mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{u}}$ and $\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{t}}$, and sample be a sampling function such that sample $(S)$ randomly generates a point from the set $S$. The random tree is then computed according to Algorithm 1, where the parameter $\alpha$ used in constructing the safe hyperrectangles is user-defined.

Once the tree $\mathscr{H}$ is constructed, and assuming there exists a point $x \in \mathscr{V}$ and associated time stamp $t$ such that $x \in \tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{\mathrm{t}, t}$, a shortest path algorithm can then be conducted over $\mathscr{T}$, connecting $p_{0}$ and $x$, and resulting in $N_{s}+1$ points $\mathrm{p}_{0}=p_{0}, \mathrm{p}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{p}_{N_{s}}=x$, with associated time stamps $\mathrm{t}_{0}=$ $0<\mathrm{t}_{1}<\cdots<\mathrm{t}_{N_{s}}$. $$
\begin{align*}

\end{align*}
$$

By construction, the points $\mathrm{p}_{0}, \mathrm{p}_{1}, \ldots, \mathrm{p}_{N_{s}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$, and associated vector radii $\mathrm{r}_{0}, \mathrm{r}_{1}, \mathrm{r}_{2}, \ldots, \mathrm{r}_{N_{s}} \in \mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}$ satisfy (73).

## B. Piecewise Bézier Curve

Once the safe tube is obtained, we compute the desired trajectory through the safe tube using Bézier curves. We assume $p_{d}$ to be a piecewise continuous function with $N_{s}$ segments, where each segment consists of $N_{p}+1$ control points. The parameter $N_{p}$ is user-defined. Let $\delta_{i}=\mathrm{t}_{i}-\mathrm{t}_{i-1}, i \in\left[1 ; N_{s}\right]$, be the duration of each segment, and $T=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}} \delta_{i}=\mathrm{t}_{N_{s}}$. Then, $p_{d}:[0, T] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{3}$ has the form

$$
p_{d}(t)= \begin{cases}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{p}} c_{1}^{i} \mathfrak{b}_{i, N_{p}}\left(\frac{t-\mathrm{t}_{0}}{\delta_{1}}\right), & t \in\left[\mathrm{t}_{0}, \mathrm{t}_{1}\right]  \tag{76}\\ \vdots & \\ \sum_{i=0}^{N_{p}} c_{N_{s}}^{i} \mathfrak{b}_{i, N_{p}}\left(\frac{t-\mathrm{t}_{N_{s}-1}}{\delta_{N_{s}}}\right), & t \in\left[\mathrm{t}_{N_{s}-1}, \mathrm{t}_{N_{s}}\right] .\end{cases}
$$

This approach incrementally increases the time spent in each safe hyperrectangle of the safe tube, or dwell time, by decreasing the value of $\alpha_{v}$. This process continues until the constraints become feasible. Let us elaborate on why the iterative scheme involving the reduction of $\alpha_{v}$ is beneficial. The key idea is as follows: decreasing $\alpha_{v}$ increases the time spent in the first safe hyperrectangle centered at $p_{0}$ (whose size remains fixed). 2: If the linear program in step 1 is feasible, then terminate and use the resulting control points and time durations $\delta_{1}, \ldots, \delta_{N_{s}}$ to obtain the desired trajectory according to (76).
3: If the linear program in step 1 is infeasible, redefine $\alpha_{v}$ as $\alpha_{v}:=\alpha_{s} \alpha_{v}$, where $\left.\alpha_{s} \in\right] 0,1[$ is a user-defined scaling parameter, recompute

This reduction then propagates forward along the sequence of waypoints, causing all subsequent safe hyperrectangles to enlarge (noting that this increase is bounded due to the boundedness of the operating domain). Moreover, the dwell time within each safe hyperrectangle increases as $\alpha_{v}$ decreases.

This simultaneous increase in the hyperrectangle radii and dwell times provides additional flexibility for trajectory synthesis via linear programming. If the velocity-scaling parameter $\alpha_{v}$ is sufficiently small, then feasibility becomes more likely since the resulting velocities and accelerations decrease (their magnitudes scale inversely with the dwell time and with the square of the dwell time, respectively), and the tracking-error bounds $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{i}, i \in\{p, v, f\}$, also decay-and do so exponentially. As a result, these quantities become sufficiently small to satisfy all imposed velocity and acceleration constraints.

The nominal trajectory starts at $p_{0}=[0,0,0]^{\top}$ and $v_{0}=0_{3}$, respectively. The quadrotor model parameters follow [13]: $J=10^{-2} \operatorname{diag}(8.20,8.45,13.77) \mathrm{kg} \cdot \mathrm{m}^{2}, m=$ 4.34 kg . The parameters associated with velocity, acceleration, and thrust bounds are set as follows: $v_{\text {max }}=[2,2,2]^{\top}$, $a_{\text {max }}=[1,1,10]^{\top}$, and $f_{\text {max }}=2 \mathrm{mg}=85.1508 \mathrm{~N}$.

## A. Gain Tuning Through Optimization

To yield nonconservative theoretical bounds, the gains are tuned by solving a nonlinear optimization problem defined in (89) using simulated annealing [44], implemented by MAT-LAB's built-in function simulannealbnd. This optimization problem aims to minimize a combination of the maximum values of the bounds $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}, \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{v}$, and $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{f}$ (which are attained at time $t=0$ due to the monotonicity with respect to time). The optimization problem is given by (the arguments are dropped for convenience)

$$
\begin{align*}
\min _{\substack{k_{p}, k_{v}, k_{R}, k_{\omega} \\
\gamma_{1}, \gamma_{2}}} & w_{1} \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}+w_{2} \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{v}+w_{3} \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{f} \\
\text { s.t. } & \underline{k} \leq k_{p}, k_{v}, k_{R}, k_{\omega} \leq \bar{k} \\
& 0<\gamma_{1}, \gamma_{2}<1 \tag{89}
\end{align*}
$$

where we set $\underline{k}=0.1, \bar{k}=30$, the weights $w_{1}=15, w_{2}=$ $w_{3}=1$, and $\left.\gamma_{1}, \gamma_{2} \in\right] 0,1\left[\right.$ are introduced to determine $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$ as

$$
\begin{align*}
& c_{1}=\gamma_{1} \min \left(\sqrt{k_{p} m}, \frac{4 m k_{p} k_{v}}{k_{v}^{2}+4 m k_{p}}\right)  \tag{90}\\
& c_{2}=\gamma_{2} \min \left(\sqrt{k_{R} \underline{\lambda}(J)}, \frac{4 \underline{\lambda}(J) k_{R} k_{\omega}}{k_{\omega}^{2}+4 \underline{\lambda}(J) k_{R}}\right) . \tag{91}
\end{align*}
$$

We set the initial guess as $k_{p}=k_{v}=10, k_{R}=k_{\omega}=$ 10, and $\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=0.5$. The optimized results are obtained as $\gamma_{1}=0.5651, \gamma_{2}=0.593, k_{p}=18.3154, k_{v}=$ $6.4322, k_{R}=22.3572$, and $k_{\omega}=1.4732$, which results in the following maximum values: $\max _{t \in \mathbb{R}_{+}} \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\left(\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{1}, \overline{\mathcal{V}}_{2}, t\right)=$ $0.34 \mathrm{~m}, \quad \max _{t \in \mathbb{R}_{+}} \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{v}\left(\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{1}, \overline{\mathcal{V}}_{2}, t\right)=0.67 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}$, and $\max _{t \in \mathbb{R}_{+}}$ $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{f}\left(\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{1}, \overline{\mathcal{V}}_{2}, t\right)=6.21 \mathrm{~N}$. In addition, the theoretical bound on thrust in (68) is $\overline{\mathcal{F}}:=\max _{t \in \mathbb{R}_{+}} \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{f}\left(\overline{\mathcal{V}}_{1}, \overline{\mathcal{V}}_{2}, t\right)+m\left\|a_{\text {max }}\right\|=$ $50.04 \mathrm{~N} \leq f_{\text {max }}$. The average computing time for obtaining the gains via optimization is 1.7682 s.

However, in cluttered or narrow environments, an overly strong goal bias often causes repeated failed extensions toward the target and can compromise the feasibility of finding a valid path. In such cases, a larger $C_{\text {sample }}$ is preferable, as it promotes broader exploration and improves the likelihood of discovering a feasible path. Nevertheless, as observed in our experiments over 100 samples of $C_{\text {sample }} \sim \mathrm{QMC}(0,1)$, the impact of $C_{\text {sample }}$ on runtime is generally small. Since our scenarios involve narrow environments, we set $C_{\text {sample }}=0.9$ in our evaluations.

Third, the velocity-scaling factor $\alpha_{s}$ impacts the ILP time for synthesis of trajectory using Bézier curves. In our setup, the initial value is $\alpha_{v}=2$, while feasibility typically requires $\alpha_{v} \approx 0.64$. ## IX. Conclusion

In this article, we proposed a control framework for a quadrotor UAV to accomplish reach-avoid tasks with formal safety guarantees, where the standard planning-tracking paradigm is adapted to account for tracking errors. The framework integrates GC theory for trajectory tracking and polynomial trajectory generation using Bézier curves, where tracking errors are accounted for during trajectory synthesis. We revisited the stability analysis of the closed-loop quadrotor system under GC, where we proved local exponential stability of the tracking error dynamics for any positive control gains and we provided tight time-varying bounds on tracking errors that can be used in planning. We also derived sufficient conditions to be imposed on the desired trajectory to ensure the well-definedness of the closed-loop quadrotor dynamics. The trajectory synthesis involved an efficient algorithm that constructs a safe tube using sampling-based planning and safe hyperrectangular set computations. The desired trajectory, represented as a piecewise continuous Bézier curve, is computed through the generated safe tube using a heuristic efficient approach, relying on iterative linear programming. Finally, we performed extensive numerical quadrotor simulations to demonstrate the proposed framework's effectiveness in reach-avoid planning scenarios.

In future work, we plan to incorporate the effects of measurement and input noises and disturbances into the safe planning framework to enable effective real-world applications. In addition, we aim to extend the proposed control synthesis to cover more complex quadrotor models (e.g., those that account for
aerodynamic drag) and more general specifications (e.g., those described by temporal logics).

This consequently indicates that $\dot{V}(t) \leq-\alpha_{0} V(t), t \in$ $I \cap\left[t_{0}, \infty[\right.$, and using the nonnegativity of $V$ and Grönwall's lemma, we have $V(t)=0, t \in I \cap\left[t_{0}, \infty[\right.$. Therefore, we may assume without loss of generality that $V(t)>0$ for all $t \in I$. Using the bound on $V_{2}(\cdot)$ in (39) and rearranging we get, for $t \in I$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{V}(t) \leq & -\left(\alpha_{0}-\alpha_{1} \sqrt{V_{2}(0)} \mathrm{e}^{-\beta t}\right) V(t) \\
& +\alpha_{2} \sqrt{V_{2}(0)} \mathrm{e}^{-\beta t} \sqrt{V(t)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we employ the following result: for a differentiable and positive $u: I \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, satisfying the Bernoulli-type nonlinear differential inequality $\dot{u}(t) \leq b(t) u(t)+k(t) \sqrt{u(t)}, t \in I$, where $b: I \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is continuous, and $k: I \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is non-negative and differentiable

$$
\sqrt{u(t)} \leq \mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{t} b(s) \mathrm{d} s}\left(\sqrt{u(0)}+\frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{t} k(s) \mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{s} b(z) \mathrm{d} z} \mathrm{~d} s\right)
$$

for all $t \in I$ (see [51, Lemma 4.1, p. 38]). Applying this result to the differential inequality satisfied by $V$, together with straightforward bounding arguments, yields (40). Finally, invoking Lemma 2(c) together with the definition of $V_{1}$ in (35), we obtain bounds on $e_{p}(t), e_{v}(t)$, and $k_{p} e_{p}(t)+k_{v} e_{v}(t)$ in terms of $\sqrt{V_{1}(t)}$ for all $t \in I$. Combining these estimates with (40) and the inequality $V_{1}(\cdot) \leq V(\cdot)$ yields the bounds (41)-(43).

## B. Proof of Theorem 5
